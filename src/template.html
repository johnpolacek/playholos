THE SCIENCE BEHIND THE VIEW

A model of what we know.

Earth, the Solar System, the measured stars, and the Milky Way occupy one continuous three-dimensional world. Scroll or use the zoom slider to travel across scales. Destination buttons move the camera through that same space; you can interrupt a journey by dragging, scrolling, or pressing Escape.

Solar System

All eight planets follow elliptical, inclined orbits computed from JPL’s approximate Keplerian elements for 1800–2050. Earth uses the Earth–Moon barycenter approximation. UTC is used in place of ephemeris time. These are educational positions, not a navigation ephemeris.

Distances stay linear. In the overview, planet disks are enlarged adaptively for visibility. Turn on True sizes for physical radii; labels still mark objects too small to see. Close-up planet surfaces use physical proportions. Earth, the Moon, and the Sun keep their relative positions throughout camera travel. Axial tilt magnitudes and rotation periods are represented; pole longitudes, cloud patterns, and surface longitude at the chosen date are illustrative. Planet shapes use spherical mean radii. The Moon uses its real mean distance and radius, with an illustrative orbit phase and fixed orbital plane. Belt particles represent a statistical distribution, not individual measured asteroids. Other moons, dwarf planets, and comets are not included.

Stars we have measured

109,400 stars from David Nash’s HYG 4.1 catalog, combining Hipparcos, Yale, and Gliese catalogs. Positions are at epoch J2000.0, transformed into Galactic coordinates. This subset includes catalog distances up to 1,000 parsecs (3,262 light-years), excluding missing-distance sentinels. It is incomplete and biased toward brighter stars; distance estimates have varying uncertainty. Proper motion is not animated.

958 named, bright, or nearby entries are searchable. Multiple stars can remain unresolved at this scale. Point sizes, brightness, and color are adapted for visibility; they do not represent stellar radii or calibrated exposure. Exoplanet orbits are not included in this first volume.

The Milky Way

The catalog sits inside a statistical reconstruction of a barred spiral disk about 100,000 light-years across, with the Sun about 26,000 light-years from the center. The bar, bulge, disk, and spiral structure follow the broad picture described by ESA’s guide to our galaxy. The generated points outside the catalog are samples of stellar density, not individual known stars. Spiral-arm geometry is illustrative, and no exact arm membership is inferred. Toggle the reconstruction off to see only the measured catalog.
